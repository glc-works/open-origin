Vietnam is the world's second-largest coffee producer after Brazil, and 97% of what it grows is robusta [1]. Much of it comes from Đắk Lắk, a province built around a plateau about 600 metres above sea level, with the city of Buôn Ma Thuột at its centre [2].

The French brought coffee to Vietnam in 1857 and opened coffee zones in the Central Highlands in the 1920s, mainly in Đắk Lắk [1]. After the war, and especially after the Đổi Mới reforms of 1986 allowed private enterprise again, four to five million people moved from the crowded lowlands to the highlands, and coffee spread fast. The growth came at a cost to the Ede and other Indigenous peoples of the highlands, some of whom were displaced, and to the forests that were cleared for it [1].

Buôn Ma Thuột coffee received a Vietnamese geographical indication in 2005, covering 100,000 hectares in eight districts of the province [3]. The province as a whole has about 200,000 hectares of coffee [3].

In the photo, robusta grows with taller black cassia trees planted among it to shelter the flowers from frost, in Cư Kuin district.
