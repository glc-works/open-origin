Coffee as a drink begins in Yemen. The earliest credible evidence of coffee drinking comes from Yemen's Sufi monasteries in the mid-15th century, where it kept the faithful awake through night prayers [3]. From the 16th to the early 19th century, coffee was shipped to the world from the Red Sea port of Mokha, which gave its name to "mocha". The coffee did not grow at the port: it came from inland Yemen, and much of it from across the sea in Ethiopia [2].

Jabal Haraz is one of those inland sources, a mountain range between Sanaa and the port of Al-Hudaydah, around the market town of Manakhah. Its villages are built like castles on rocky peaks, and its slopes are cut into terraces held up by stone walls, some several metres high. On them grow millet, lentils, alfalfa, qat and large areas of coffee. Haraz is one of the main growing areas of Mocha coffee, and it has been on Yemen's tentative list for UNESCO World Heritage since 2002 [1].

Most Yemeni coffee is still made the old way. Cherries are dried whole, often on rooftops; the dried husks are split with millstones, and the beans are winnowed by hand [4]. The trees are old local varieties, and a genetic study found a group of them unlike coffee anywhere else, now promoted under the name "Yemenia" [4]. Reviewers find the best of them full of flowers, dark berries, chocolate and an incense-like cedar [4].

War and conflict have made exporting hard, and in the early 2000s it looked as if Yemen might stop exporting coffee altogether. Specialty buyers who pay for rare, distinct coffees have since given the old terraces a second chance [4].
