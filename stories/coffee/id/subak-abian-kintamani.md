The world knows the subak through Bali's terraced rice fields, irrigated together by the community. Subak Abian is its counterpart for dry land: customary groups of garden farmers who gather at the temple, share work, and agree on awig-awig, rules that bind every member.

In Kintamani, these rules govern how coffee is grown. Chemical fertiliser and pesticides are banned, and manure and compost are used instead. Cherries may only be picked when red. Coffee grows with citrus, bananas and vegetables, so the gardens keep earning outside the coffee harvest.

This movement was not brought in from outside. It grew from Tri Hita Karana, the philosophy of harmony between people, nature and God, practised every day. When Kintamani applied for its Geographical Indication in 2008, the subak rules became the basis of its quality standard. For farmers elsewhere in the archipelago, Subak Abian shows that custom can be a living quality system.
