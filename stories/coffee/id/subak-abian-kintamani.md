The world knows the subak through Bali's terraced rice fields, irrigated together by the community. Subak Abian is its counterpart for dry land: customary groups of garden farmers that look after social and religious life and also act as an economic group for the growers [1].

In Kintamani, the groups' rules govern how coffee is grown. Chemical fertiliser and pesticides are not used [1][2]. Cherries may only be picked when red, and a member who picks green cherries pays a fine multiplied by the number of farmers in the group; a serious breach can lead to exclusion from the village [2]. Many families keep orange trees beside their coffee [4].

This way of farming grew from Tri Hita Karana, the philosophy of harmony between people, nature and God [1]. Researchers who studied Kintamani in the early 2000s saw the Subak Abian as the natural base for a Geographical Indication [1], and in 2008 an association of local farmers and processors obtained it [3]. For farmers elsewhere in the archipelago, Subak Abian shows that custom can be a living quality system.
