In the shadow of Mount Batur, Kintamani farmers manage their gardens through the Subak Abian, a customary organisation for dryland farming [2][3]. It follows the philosophy of Tri Hita Karana: harmony with God, with other people, and with nature [3].

Subak rules forbid chemical fertiliser and require picking only ripe red cherries, and breaking them brings a fine [2]. The arabica grows between about 1,000 and 1,500 metres and is mostly wet-processed [3]. Many families also grow oranges, which for years were worth more than coffee [4]. Tasters have found a lemon or orange note in the cup since the first studies of the area [3][4].

In 2008, Kintamani Bali Arabica became the first Indonesian product to receive a Geographical Indication [1]. Dozens of other regional coffees have followed [5].
